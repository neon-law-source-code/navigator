# LSAT Study Aid

Use this page as a small, repeatable practice loop: choose one question, explain your reasoning in your own words, then
review what changed when you compare your explanation with the credited answer.

LSAT is a trademark of the Law School Admission Council (LSAC). This is an independent study aid and is not affiliated
with or endorsed by LSAC.

These prompts are practice scaffolds, not official LSAC questions, a score prediction, or legal advice.

## Five-day study plan

Keep one page of notes for the week. Spend more time explaining why an answer works than collecting answer letters.

### Day 1: Map the argument

Read one argument slowly. Identify its conclusion, its evidence, and the job each sentence does.

**Copy this prompt:**

> Explain this argument as if you were teaching it to a classmate. Name the conclusion, the evidence, and the job each
> sentence does. Then state one detail you would need to check before trusting the conclusion.

---

After writing, compare your explanation with the credited answer and mark the first place where your map differs.

### Day 2: Find the assumption

Return to the same argument and look for the bridge it needs in order to reach its conclusion.

**Copy this prompt:**

> What must be true for the evidence to support the conclusion? Write one assumption, then imagine a new fact that would
> weaken that assumption without changing the facts already stated.

---

Treat the assumption as a testable bridge, not as a restatement of the conclusion.

### Day 3: Test the choices

Before choosing an answer, predict the kind of statement the question is asking you to find.

**Copy this prompt:**

> Predict the answer category before reading the choices. For each choice, write one sentence explaining whether it does
> the requested job, and cross out the choices that answer a different question.

---

The useful review is the reason an attractive wrong choice fails, not just the letter of the credited choice.

### Day 4: Explain a miss

Use one missed question as a small writing exercise instead of moving on immediately.

**Copy this prompt:**

> Explain your first choice, the credited choice, and the exact sentence where the two paths split. Name the reading
> habit that led you away from the credited choice and one adjustment you will test next time.

---

Keep the adjustment narrow enough to try on the next question; a vague promise to “read better” is not a study plan.

### Day 5: Add a timer last

Repeat the loop with a modest time limit only after your untimed explanation is clear.

**Copy this prompt:**

> Solve the question once under the time limit, then explain it again without the timer. Compare the two explanations
> and identify whether speed changed your structure, your evidence tracking, or your answer-choice review.

---

Use timing as information about your process, not as a reason to skip the explanation that makes the process improvable.

## Prompt bank

Use one prompt at a time. Write the explanation before checking a solution so the review shows your actual reasoning.

### Explain the gap

> Separate the conclusion from the evidence, then describe the gap between them in one sentence. What additional fact
> would make the conclusion easier to accept, and what fact would make it harder to accept?

---

This prompt is about structure: the added facts should change the support, not merely repeat a sentence from the
argument.

### Try the strongest objection

> Write the strongest fair objection to the argument you can. Then answer your objection using only information the
> passage gives you or a clearly stated assumption.

---

A strong objection makes the reasoning visible even when it does not change the credited answer.

### Review a tempting choice

> Pick the answer choice that tempted you most. Explain the job it appears to do, the job the question actually asks
> for, and the smallest difference between those two jobs.

---

The smallest difference is often more useful to remember than a long list of trap names.

## Keep the loop useful

Do not turn the page into a score log. Keep the notes that help you recognize a reasoning pattern, and discard anything
you do not want to keep; nothing typed on this page is submitted or saved by Neon Law.

If you are curious about the systems a law firm uses to turn reviewed work into a durable record, [read about Neon Law
Navigator](/navigator) as optional context; it is separate from this study aid.

### A final check

Before you finish, say the argument's conclusion, the support it relies on, and the reason the credited answer does the
requested job. Then choose the next question while the lesson is still clear.
